How Have San Diego's Temperature Extremes Shifted Under Different Emission Scenarios?

This project explores how San Diego's annual minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures have shifted from 1850 to 2100 using CMIP6 climate model data. We compare historical temperatures with moderate and high emissions projections to understand how local temperature extremes may change.

Dataset

We use Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) climate model data to study temperature patterns in San Diego. The raw data includes monthly temperature values measured in Kelvin, which we transformed into annual minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures in Celsius.

Dataset: CMIP6 climate model temperature data
Variables: Annual minimum, mean, and maximum temperature
Scenarios: Historical, SSP2-4.5 moderate emissions, and SSP5-8.5 high emissions
Region of interest: San Diego, California
View CMIP6 Dataset

Research Question

How have San Diego's temperature extremes, including annual minimums and maximums, shifted from 1850 to 2100 under moderate versus high emission scenarios?

We compare historical temperatures with two future emissions pathways to examine whether San Diego's annual temperature extremes become warmer, more variable, or increasingly different between moderate and high emissions conditions.

Visualizations

This section contains two D3 graphs. The first graph provides a spatial view of California temperature extremes across years, while the second graph focuses on San Diego by comparing historical temperature trends with moderate and high emissions projections from 1850 to 2100.

Graph 1: California Extreme Temperature Patterns by Year

Graph 2: San Diego Temperature Extremes and Future Scenarios

This D3 line chart compares San Diego's annual minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures across historical data and future climate scenarios. Users can toggle historical, SSP2-4.5 moderate emissions, and SSP5-8.5 high emissions to see how temperature extremes shift over time.

Climate Context

From “Sunny San Diego” to Climate Change

San Diego is often described as having stable, warm, and sunny weather. As UCSD students, this local reputation motivated us to investigate whether that perception still holds when we look at long-term temperature data and future climate projections.

Historical and Future Scenarios

Our project compares historical temperature data from 1850 to 2014 with projected data from 2015 to 2100. By separating SSP2-4.5 moderate emissions and SSP5-8.5 high emissions, we can examine how different emissions pathways may lead to different temperature outcomes.

Annual Minimums and Maximums

Instead of only showing average temperature, our main graph also includes annual minimum and maximum temperatures. This helps us study both sides of temperature extremes and see whether San Diego's coldest and hottest yearly values shift upward over time.

Why Interaction Matters

The interactive toggles allow viewers to compare historical, moderate-emissions, and high-emissions lines directly. This makes it easier to see when the projection scenarios begin to diverge and how much more extreme the high-emissions pathway becomes by 2100.

Write-up

Motivation

As students going to UCSD, we often assume that the advertised idea of “sunny San Diego” is accurate. This was our initial assumption when attending campus, and we wanted to further investigate the validity of this perceived stable, warm, and sunny weather. We also wanted to understand how average temperature has changed over time.

Another question within this investigation is how temperature extremes, including the coldest and hottest periods, have changed and how these patterns might differ under different emissions scenarios. This led us to our final question: How have San Diego's temperature extremes, including annual minimums and maximums, shifted from 1850 to 2100 under moderate versus high emission scenarios?

Data Transformations

When we initially loaded the raw Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) data, it provided historical monthly temperature values measured in Kelvin. To create a usable and more understandable dataset, we transformed the data in several ways.

First, we aggregated the monthly values to calculate annual minimum, maximum, and mean temperatures. Then, we converted Kelvin to Celsius so the values would be easier to interpret. Although the United States commonly uses Fahrenheit, Celsius provides a more universally accepted measurement for climate data.

Lastly, we categorized the data into three scenarios before converting it into CSV files: historical data from 1850 to 2014, SSP2-4.5 moderate emissions from 2015 to 2100, and SSP5-8.5 high emissions from 2015 to 2100. Separating moderate and high emissions was important because it allowed us to visualize how different future emissions pathways may affect San Diego's temperature extremes.

Design Rationale

In our visualization, we used several visual encodings to highlight our findings. The x-axis is labeled from 1850 to 2100, allowing us to show both historical observed data and projected temperature changes under different emissions conditions. The y-axis is labeled in Celsius, using a linear scale to make the temperature values easy to compare.

We used solid lines to represent annual mean temperature. Different colors distinguish the historical, moderate-emissions, and high-emissions scenarios. We used dashed lines to represent annual minimum and maximum temperatures. These dashed lines contrast with the solid mean lines and emphasize how the yearly extremes deviate from the average.

Finally, we included a vertical dashed line to mark the separation between historical data and projected data. This helps viewers understand when the graph shifts from observed climate data to future climate scenarios.

Development Process

Our team divided the project based on writing, visual design, and website implementation. James Vidal worked on the written explanation, Kaitlyn Tam and Vivian Zhu worked on the visual graphs, and Zhixiang Zhang worked on building and organizing the website.

The most time-consuming parts were preparing the climate data, converting it into a format that could be used in D3, and integrating multiple visualizations into one webpage. We also spent time making sure the interactive graphs could be loaded from static files so the final project would work correctly on GitHub Pages.
